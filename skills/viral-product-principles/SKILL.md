# Viral Product Principles — Universal

## Purpose

Use sharp heuristics to make products easier to understand, buy, use, remember, and share.

This skill is inspired by Marc Lou's **32 Principles of a Viral Product**. The original material is a product-builder manifesto, not a universal scientific framework. Apply it as a set of hypotheses and provocations, not commandments.

## Principle hierarchy

The heuristics cluster into six useful lenses:

1. **Simplicity**
2. **Clarity and copy**
3. **Product proof**
4. **Pricing and purchase**
5. **Shareability/distribution**
6. **Memorability and differentiation**

Do not mechanically apply all 32 ideas on every task.

Select the **3–5 most relevant** principles for each review pass.

## Rule zero: patterns, not laws

Some Marc Lou principles are intentionally provocative.

Examples include strong opinions on:
- free plans;
- subscription pricing;
- charging more;
- visual simplicity;
- competitive positioning.

Do not treat such claims as universal truths.

Before applying a principle, ask:
- What business model is this?
- What market stage is this?
- What is the user's purchase frequency?
- What is the marginal cost?
- What is the sales motion?
- Is network effect important?
- Is a free tier part of distribution?
- Does the user's evidence support or contradict the heuristic?

Use the principle to challenge assumptions, not to replace analysis.

## Lens 1 — Simplicity

Viral products are easier to explain and easier to use.

Audit:
- Can the product be described in one short sentence?
- Is the primary use case obvious?
- Is the first screen overloaded?
- Are there too many visual priorities?
- Are there too many colors competing for attention?
- Are secondary features stealing attention from the core action?
- Does navigation reveal unnecessary complexity too early?
- Can a feature, field, step, plan, or visual element be removed?

Prefer strong hierarchy over decorative variety.

## Lens 2 — Clarity and copy

Concrete language spreads better than vague language.

Prefer:
- numbers where they are meaningful;
- outcomes over abstract adjectives;
- customer vocabulary over internal jargon;
- specific proof over broad claims;
- direct verbs;
- short explanations for the core value.

Ask:
- Could a user repeat the value proposition to someone else?
- Does the headline explain a real outcome?
- Are words like "powerful", "seamless", "innovative", or "smart" hiding a lack of specificity?
- Is the CTA explicit about what happens next?

Do not use fabricated numbers.

## Lens 3 — Show the product

When possible, demonstrate rather than merely describe.

Useful proof can include:
- live product interaction;
- screenshot;
- before/after;
- generated result;
- interactive demo;
- sample output;
- real workflow;
- concise video;
- real data example.

The goal is to reduce uncertainty.

Do not add a demo if it distracts from an already-understood product.

## Lens 4 — Pricing and purchase

Make the buying decision understandable.

Audit:
- Is pricing discoverable?
- Is the paid value obvious?
- Are packages meaningfully different?
- Are limits comprehensible?
- Does the pricing model match the way value is delivered?
- Is the primary purchase action easy to find?
- Are objections answered before checkout?
- Is the product underpricing itself out of fear rather than evidence?

Treat "no free plan", "no subscription", and "charge more" as testable hypotheses.

For each pricing recommendation provide:
- rationale;
- target segment;
- expected behavior;
- metric;
- downside;
- reversible test.

## Lens 5 — Shareability and distribution

A viral product gives users a reason or mechanism to talk about it.

Look for:
- outputs users naturally want to share;
- collaboration;
- public artifacts;
- identity/status value;
- referral loops;
- templates;
- embeds;
- links;
- generated content;
- visible attribution where appropriate;
- moments of delight worth showing others.

Do not force sharing or expose private content by default.

A share loop must preserve user control.

## Lens 6 — Memorability and differentiation

A product needs a story people can recall.

Ask:
- What is the one thing a user would remember tomorrow?
- Can the product be compared to a familiar alternative in a useful way?
- Is there a sharp, defensible point of view?
- Is there one surprising capability or interaction?
- Does the product look and sound like every AI startup?
- Is the differentiator meaningful or just decorative?

Avoid novelty for novelty's sake.

## Hero / first-screen audit

The first screen should usually answer enough of:
- What is this?
- Who is it for?
- What result does it create?
- What does it look like?
- Why should I believe it?
- What should I do next?

Do not force all answers into one giant text block. Use hierarchy and proof.

## Product audit workflow

1. Identify the product's core job.
2. Define the target user.
3. Identify the current acquisition/purchase path.
4. Find the biggest source of confusion.
5. Choose 3–5 relevant viral-product principles.
6. Recommend concrete changes.
7. State what to remove, not only what to add.
8. Define metric(s).
9. State a counterargument for each strong recommendation.
10. Identify trust/privacy risks.
11. Rank experiments by impact and reversibility.

## Build mode

When creating a new product or feature:
- start with one clear promise;
- design the shortest path to proof;
- expose the core action;
- delay secondary complexity;
- keep copy repeatable;
- make the output demonstrable;
- identify a natural sharing mechanism only if the product supports one;
- create a simple initial pricing hypothesis;
- define what evidence would invalidate the hypothesis.

## Critique mode

Use this output:

### 1. Core message
One sentence describing what the product currently communicates.

### 2. 3–5 relevant principles
| Principle/lens | Current issue | Recommended move | Why | Metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### 3. Remove / simplify
List the highest-value deletions.

### 4. Viral/share opportunity
Only if a natural one exists.

### 5. Pricing hypothesis
Only if relevant.

### 6. Counterarguments
Where the Marc-style heuristic could be wrong for this specific product.

### 7. Next experiment
The smallest high-information test.

## Trust and anti-dark-pattern rules

Never:
- fabricate social proof;
- create false scarcity;
- fake urgency;
- misrepresent prices;
- hide recurring billing;
- obstruct cancellation;
- expose user content for virality;
- coerce invites;
- optimize a click metric at the expense of informed choice.

Virality should come from product value, identity, utility, or collaboration—not deception.

## Evidence discipline

Label recommendations as:
- **Observed** — directly supported by product/repository/data;
- **Heuristic** — suggested by the Marc-style principles;
- **Hypothesis** — plausible but untested;
- **Validated** — supported by meaningful user/business evidence.

Do not present a heuristic as validated evidence.
